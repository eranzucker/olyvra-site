How it counts

Any part of a day in the US counts as a day. The test adds all days this calendar year, one third of last year's days and one sixth of the days from the year before. If that total reaches 183 and you spent at least 31 days in the US this year, the test is met and you may be treated as a US resident for tax purposes, unless an exception applies.

Some days do not count, so leave them out of your trips: commuting days from a home in Canada or Mexico, layovers of under 24 hours between two places outside the US, days unable to leave because of a medical condition that started in the US, and days as an exempt individual, such as many F, J, M and Q visa holders. The United States here means the 50 states and DC, not Puerto Rico or other territories.

Meeting the test is not the final word. If you were in the US fewer than 183 days this year, the closer connection exception (Form 8840) may still apply, and a tax treaty can also decide where you are resident.

This is a day count, not a residency determination; confirm your status with the IRS rules or a qualified tax adviser.

Questions

Why is the total not a whole number?

Last year's days count as a third each and the year before's as a sixth, so the total can include fractions. The test is met when it reaches 183.

I met the test. Am I a US tax resident?

Not necessarily. Exceptions such as the closer connection exception or a tax treaty can still apply. Check the IRS rules or ask a tax adviser.

Does a layover in the US count?

Not if you are in transit between two places outside the US for less than 24 hours. Leave those days out of your trips.
